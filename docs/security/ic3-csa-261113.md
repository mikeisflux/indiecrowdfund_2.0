Threat Overview of ARTEX Autonomous Penetration
Testing Framework

Summary
The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) is releasing this FLASH to highlight the tactics, techniques, and
procedures (TTPs) and indicators of compromise (IOCs) associated with the ARTEX autonomous
penetration testing framework. ARTEX is an open-source, large language model (LLM)-driven agentic
platform that threat actors have weaponized to automate multiple phases of cyber intrusions at machine
speed, from reconnaissance through data exfiltration. Since at least August 2026, a variety of malicious
cyber actors have used ARTEX to conduct campaigns targeting organizations globally across multiple
sectors, including financial services, government, technology, defense, semiconductor, energy,
healthcare, manufacturing, telecommunications, and legal industries.1,2,3,4,5

ARTEX significantly reduces the time required to chain attack techniques, with some intrusions
progressing from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours.² The framework enables threat
actors with minimal technical expertise to conduct complex, multi-target operations simultaneously,
fundamentally accelerating attack velocity and expanding operational scale. Notable campaigns include
financially motivated attacks against South Korean financial institutions resulting in exfiltration of personal
information from approximately 67,000 individuals,6,7,8 and a supply chain compromise affecting
ConnectWise Cloud Backup customers that enabled access to more than 2,250 downstream tenants.2

Following public disclosure of malicious usage, the original ARTEX developer removed the GitHub
repository in October 2026.3 However, the framework continues to proliferate through community forks,
localized variations, archived copies, and a commercial version (ARTEX PRO), ensuring continued
availability to threat actors.3,10 As of early November 2026, researchers identified over 1,600 publicly
exposed ARTEX console instances, with total deployments likely significantly higher when including
systems behind firewalls or in private networks.4

The FBI encourages organizations—particularly those operating internet-exposed applications, self-
service portals, cloud infrastructure, and partner/supplier systems—to implement the recommendations
in the Mitigations section to reduce the likelihood and impact of ARTEX-enabled intrusions.

Threat
ARTEX is an autonomous, multi-agent penetration testing platform written in Go.3,4 The framework
translates high-level tactical goals described in natural language into actionable machine commands
executed by specialized super intelligence (SI) agents, requiring minimal human interaction beyond initial
objective setting.2,3 ARTEX does not include its own SI model but integrates with external LLM providers,
including frontier models (Anthropic Claude, OpenAI GPT) and open-source or Chinese-origin models
(DeepSeek, GLM, Grok, Kimi).3,6,7,9

Analysis of attacker-controlled ARTEX instances shows Chinese models are most prevalent, with
DeepSeek Flash family models dominating usage due to their balance of capabilities, relatively low cost,
and lack of observable guardrails when framed for penetration testing. 7,9

Originally released on GitHub by developer “Autumn-27” in July 2026, ARTEX gained rapid adoption,
reaching an estimated 1,500–2,000 GitHub stars within 10 weeks.4 The developer stated the tool was
intended for authorized security testing within defined scopes and made the repository unavailable on
October 8, 2026, following reports of threat actor abuse.3,6 Despite this action, ARTEX remains widely
accessible through independent routes and mirrors on code-sharing platforms.3,4,9

Observed Capabilities and Potential Uses
Based on observed capabilities and ongoing threat actor adoption, ARTEX presents the following risks:

   •   Accelerated Multi-Target Operations: SI agents provide automation for the time-consuming
       phases of enumeration and exploitation; small threat groups can run concurrent intrusion
       campaigns at a scale previously unavailable to them.
   •   Exploitation of Overlooked Attack Surfaces: ARTEX excels at identifying and exploiting peripheral
       systems receiving less security attention than flagship applications, including partner and supplier
       portals, mobile backends, employee applications, sales-support systems, legacy services, third-
       party integrations, and applications created across different business units.1
   •   Supply Chain Compromise: Agents have demonstrated capability to identify and exploit supply
       chain attack vectors, including modifying iOS application distribution infrastructure via exposed
       cloud credentials.9 Autonomous agents can systematically map trust relationships between
       organizations. By identifying and compromising weaker third-party vendors, CI/CD pipelines, or
       integrated software providers, attackers can leverage ARTEX to pivot from a peripheral supplier
       directly into high-value upstream enterprise networks.
   •   Reduced Barrier to Entry: ARTEX lowers technical barriers for conducting complex intrusions,
       enabling threat actors with minimal expertise to execute sophisticated campaigns. 3,9

Information Requested
The FBI encourages recipients of this document to report information concerning suspicious or criminal
activity to their local FBI field office at www.fbi.gov/contact-us/field-offices or the FBI Internet Crime
Complaint Center (IC3) at www.ic3.gov.

If reporting an incident involving ARTEX or similar agentic SI frameworks, please provide the following
information:

1. ARTEX Infrastructure Artifacts: Source code or screenshots of suspected ARTEX console interfaces
and ARTEX configuration files

2. Authentication and Access Logs: Service principal sign-in logs showing unusual source networks,
ASNs, hosting providers, or geographies; user agent strings from suspicious authentication attempts; IP
login information and associated timestamps; evidence of authentication from previously unseen
infrastructure

3. Application and API Activity: Microsoft Graph activity; Microsoft Entra audit records; Microsoft 365
audit records showing reconnaissance; sequential lookups or unusually high numbers of records
accessed per session on partner/employee portals; rate limit violations or volumetric anomalies in API
calls

4. LLM and SI Infrastructure Indicators: Outbound connections to LLM API endpoints; evidence of self-
hosted LLM gateway or reseller software (CLIProxyAPI, New API); unfamiliar LLM proxies or resellers in
egress logs; token consumption patterns or API billing anomalies

5. Cloud and Container Activity: AWS IAM activity logs; Azure ARM deployment validation dry-runs across
multiple resource types; test blob write/delete operations in cloud storage with sub-second timing;
evidence of compromised Kudu/SCM credentials or unusual Kudu API activity; container orchestration
logs; cloud metadata service queries; unexpected IAM role assumptions, PassRole operations, or instance
profile modifications

6. Exploitation and Persistence Artifacts: Web shells; evidence of BLOODALCHEMY components; open-
source RAT indicators (WISP, vshell, AdaptixC2); newly created accounts with naming patterns;

Recommendations
The FBI recommends organizations take the steps below to improve their security posture in response to
ARTEX and similar agentic SI frameworks.

Immediate Actions

Reduce External Attack Surface:

• Inventory all internet-facing applications, including peripheral systems: partner and supplier portals,
mobile backends, employee applications, sales-support systems, legacy services, third-party integrations,
and applications created across different business units.1,11

• Restrict cloud administration, network device management, application publishing interfaces
(Kudu/SCM endpoints), hypervisors, backup platforms, and other sensitive services to trusted networks,
private connectivity, or dedicated administrative paths.⁵

• Disable basic publishing authentication where not required; restrict access to deployment and
publishing credentials; restrict SCM access to approved management networks or deployment
infrastructure.⁵

• Consider deployment models such as run-from-package that reduce direct modification of deployed
application files.⁵

• Establish governance around public IP allocation; require architecture review before workloads or
management interfaces are exposed directly to the internet.5

• Move enterprise applications behind Zero Trust access solutions to eliminate external exposure where
feasible.7

Harden Identity and Access:

• Apply least privilege to multi-tenant application permissions and consent grants.2

• Monitor credential additions, privilege assignments, and anomalous app-only activity, especially to
applications with high privileged permissions and scopes.2

• Review every Microsoft 365 tenant where affected ConnectWise applications are currently or were
previously installed or authorized, even if integration no longer in use; search by application ID rather than
display name.2

• Review successful and failed service-principal sign-ins separately; investigate unfamiliar sources and
activity outside expected vendor infrastructure (do not limit review to published IPs).2

• Review Microsoft Entra audit records for application/service principal credential additions; new
applications/service principals/owners; app-role assignments and consent grants; privileged role
assignments; changes to users/groups/authentication methods/Conditional Access (CA). If suspicious
activity is confirmed, revoke unnecessary permissions and rotate credentials.2

• Require certificate manager or CA approval for higher-risk enrollment scenarios; enable Extended
Protection for Authentication on CA web interfaces where supported; monitor unusual certificate
enrollment involving privileged users or unexpected requesting systems.5

Strengthen Application Protections:

• Put guardrails around high-impact business APIs: rate limits, recipient limits, transaction thresholds,
abuse controls appropriate to function; alert when application operates materially outside normal
volume.5

• On partner and employee portals, alert on sequential lookups and unusually high numbers of records
accessed per session; apply rate limits to affected endpoints; enforce server-side authorization for access
to customer records and limit responses to fields required for the operation.7

• Avoid returning detailed stack traces, compiler messages, SQL errors, internal paths, framework details
to unauthenticated users; keep detailed troubleshooting information in internal logs. 5

• Review application configurations, environment variables, infrastructure-as-code, deployment scripts,
CI/CD pipelines, databases for embedded secrets; implement continuous scanning of repositories,
container images, build artifacts, pipelines for exposed secrets; rotate exposed credentials promptly.5

Preserve Evidence and Respond:

• Preserve available Microsoft Entra sign-in and audit records, Microsoft Purview Audit records, SIEM data,
previously configured Microsoft Graph telemetry; do not delay urgent containment while waiting for
exports.2

• Review activity starting from at least October 1, 2026, for ConnectWise-related activity; extend service-
principal sign-in review and investigate earlier related activity including failed authentications and volume
anomalies.2

unauthorized credentials, grants, owners, assignments, identities after preserving evidence. 2

• Keep affected ConnectWise application connections disabled until ConnectWise provides formal

and evidence before irreversible changes).2
